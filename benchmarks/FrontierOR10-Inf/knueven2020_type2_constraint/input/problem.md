# Problem Description

A power system operator manages a fleet of thermal generators over a planning horizon of consecutive hourly time periods (typically 48 hours). Each generator is characterized by a minimum stable power output, a maximum power output, a ramp-up rate limiting the increase in power output from one period to the next, a ramp-down rate limiting the decrease, a startup ramp limit specifying the maximum power the generator can produce in the first period it comes online, a shutdown ramp limit specifying the maximum power it can produce in the last period before going offline, a minimum up-time stating the fewest consecutive periods the generator must remain on once started, and a minimum down-time stating the fewest consecutive periods it must remain off once shut down. Each generator also has a no-load cost incurred per period whenever it is online, a piecewise linear production cost curve defined by breakpoints and marginal cost slopes, and a downtime-dependent startup cost function (cheaper after shorter off-periods, more expensive after longer off-periods, with a finite number of cost levels). The system specifies an electricity demand for each period and a reserve requirement for each period.

For each generator in each period, the operator must decide whether the generator is on or off, the power output of the generator, and the maximum power the generator can make available for reserve in that period. When a generator is on, its power output must be at least its minimum stable output and at most its maximum output, and the power available for reserve must be at least as large as the actual power output and at most the maximum output. When a generator is off, its power output and reserve-available power must both be zero.

Generator on/off decisions over time are constrained by ramping and minimum up/down times. Between consecutive periods in which a generator remains on, the change in its power output from one period to the next must not exceed the ramp-up rate, and any decrease must not exceed the ramp-down rate. When a generator transitions from off to on between two consecutive periods, its power output in the first on-period is bounded above by the startup ramp limit. When a generator transitions from on to off between two consecutive periods, its power output in the last on-period is bounded above by the shutdown ramp limit. Once a generator starts running (i.e., its on/off status changes from off in the previous period to on in the current period), it must remain on for at least as many consecutive periods as its minimum up-time; symmetrically, once it shuts down, it must remain off for at least as many consecutive periods as its minimum down-time. A generator that was already on before the planning horizon began and has not yet satisfied its minimum up-time must remain on for the required number of remaining periods; a generator that was already off and has not yet satisfied its minimum down-time must remain off.

In every period, the total power output of all generators must exactly equal the system demand. In every period, the total power available for reserve across all generators must be at least the system demand plus the reserve requirement.

The goal is to minimize the total operating cost over the planning horizon. For each generator and each period in which the generator is on, the operating cost for that period consists of a no-load cost plus the piecewise linear production cost evaluated at the generator's power output. In addition, whenever a generator transitions from off to on, a startup cost is incurred that depends on how many consecutive periods the generator had been off before restarting, determined by the generator's downtime-dependent startup cost function. The objective sums these no-load, production, and startup costs across all generators and all periods.


## Additional binding constraints
In addition to all requirements above, every generator must remain off in every time period. External net injection must equal zero in every time period.
