# Problem Description

A delivery company operates a fleet of identical vehicles from a single depot to serve a set of customers. Each customer has a known positive demand, a location specified by coordinates in a plane, and a time window during which service must begin. The depot also has a time window representing the planning horizon within which all vehicles must depart and return. Each vehicle has a fixed carrying capacity. The number of available vehicles may be set to an upper bound computed as the sum, over all customers, of the ceiling of each customer's demand divided by the vehicle capacity.

The input data for each instance specifies the number of customers, the vehicle capacity, the depot location and its time window, and for each customer an identifier, coordinates, demand quantity, time window, and service time. A symmetric distance and travel time matrix is provided for all pairs of locations including the depot. Distances are Euclidean, computed with one decimal place and truncated. Travel times equal distances, and the triangle inequality holds for both costs and travel times. The travel time along an arc from one location to another includes the service time at the origin location. Service times are independent of the quantity delivered.

Each vehicle travels a route that starts at the depot, visits some subset of customers, and returns to the depot. A direct arc between two locations exists only if a vehicle that begins service at the origin at the start of the origin's time window would arrive at the destination no later than the end of the destination's time window. An arc directly from the departure depot to the return depot also exists, representing an idle vehicle. The cost of traversing an arc equals the distance between the two locations.

Unlike the classical vehicle routing problem, a customer's demand may be fulfilled by deliveries from multiple vehicles. Each vehicle that visits a customer delivers some quantity to that customer, and the total quantity delivered to each customer across all vehicles must be at least the customer's demand. The quantity a single vehicle can deliver to a customer is at most the minimum of the customer's demand and the vehicle capacity, and a vehicle can deliver a positive quantity to a customer only if that vehicle's route actually visits that customer. The total quantity carried by each vehicle across all its deliveries must not exceed the vehicle capacity.

Each vehicle's route must form a simple path from the departure depot to the return depot, meaning that at every customer node the number of incoming arcs used by that vehicle equals the number of outgoing arcs, exactly one arc leaves the departure depot, and exactly one arc enters the return depot. When a vehicle traverses an arc, its visit start time at the destination must be no earlier than its visit start time at the origin plus the travel time on that arc. At every node, the vehicle's visit start time must fall within that node's time window; a vehicle may arrive before the time window opens and wait, but service cannot begin after the window closes.

The total number of vehicles used equals the number of vehicles whose routes leave the departure depot to visit at least one customer. This count must be an integer and must fall between the minimum number of vehicles needed to cover the total demand, computed as the ceiling of total demand divided by vehicle capacity, and the total number of available vehicles.

The goal is to determine routes for the vehicles, the quantity each vehicle delivers to each customer it visits, and the visit schedule at each location so as to minimize the total travel cost, defined as the sum of arc costs over all arcs used by all vehicles.
