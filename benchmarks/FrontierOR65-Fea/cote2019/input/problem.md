# Problem Description

A delivery company operates a fleet of K identical vehicles from a central depot to serve a set of n customers. The depot and customers form the vertices of a complete undirected graph, where each edge has a known travel cost. Each vehicle has a rectangular loading area of height H and width W, and a maximum weight capacity Q. Every customer must be visited by exactly one vehicle, and every vehicle route must start and end at the depot. All K vehicles must be used.

Each customer requires delivery of a set of two-dimensional rectangular items. Each item has a height, a width, and a weight. Some items are deterministic, meaning their dimensions and weight are known with certainty when routes are planned. Other items are stochastic: their exact height, width, and weight are not known at planning time but are instead drawn from a discrete probability distribution with a finite number of possible realizations, each having a known probability. The probabilities across all realizations of a given item sum to one. Every realization of every item has height at most H, width at most W, and weight at most Q. The expected area covered by a customer's items is computed as the probability-weighted sum of the product of height and width across all realizations of all items belonging to that customer. The expected weight of a customer's items is computed analogously as the probability-weighted sum of item weights.

The company must select which edges to traverse, thereby determining the assignment of customers to vehicles and the ordering of visits within each route. Each customer must be connected to exactly two edges in the solution (arriving and departing), ensuring it appears on exactly one route. The total number of edges connecting the depot to customers equals twice the number of vehicles, reflecting that each vehicle departs from and returns to the depot. For any subset of customers, the number of edges used strictly among those customers must not exceed the size of the subset minus the maximum of two quantities: the ceiling of the ratio of their total expected area to the loading area H times W, and the ceiling of the ratio of their total expected weight to Q. This prevents the formation of routes that would, in expectation, exceed either the area or weight capacity of a single vehicle, and simultaneously eliminates subtours.

Items are packed into the vehicle's rectangular loading area without rotation and without overlap. At each delivery stop along a route, the current customer's items must be unloadable by pulling them out of the vehicle from the rear (top of the packing) without moving items belonging to customers who have not yet been served. A route cannot be completed if no valid packing satisfying these two-dimensional orthogonal packing and unloading conditions exists. For deterministic routes, this is checked directly against the known item dimensions. For routes containing stochastic items, a scenario is a joint realization of all stochastic items on that route, and the route is evaluated across all possible scenarios. Any route that cannot be packed under any possible scenario is prohibited entirely from the solution.

When item sizes become known just before departure and it turns out that the items assigned to a vehicle cannot all be packed into the loading area, some customers are left unserved. The expected penalty cost of a route is the per-customer penalty amount multiplied by the probability-weighted sum of the number of unserved customers across all scenarios for that route. The total expected penalty cost of a solution is the sum of penalty costs over all routes. The goal is to minimize the sum of total travel cost over all edges used plus the total expected penalty cost across all routes.
