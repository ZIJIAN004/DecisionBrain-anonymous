# Problem Description

An air traffic management authority oversees a network of airports over a single day divided into equally spaced time periods. The input data specifies a set of flights, a set of airports, a set of time periods, and a set of weather scenarios. Each flight has a designated departure airport, arrival airport, a requested departure time period, a requested arrival time period, and a scheduled en-route duration equal to the difference between the requested arrival and departure periods. Each flight also has a minimum and maximum allowable en-route duration, a maximum allowable departure delay, a maximum allowable arrival delay, a per-period departure delay cost, and a per-period arrival delay cost. Two types of flight-pair connections are given: a first set of connected pairs covers both aircraft turnaround connections and passenger itinerary connections, each specifying a minimum number of time periods that must separate the arrival of the first flight from the departure of the second; a second set covers only aircraft turnaround connections, each again with its own minimum connecting time. Each airport has a piecewise-linear capacity envelope that limits the combination of departures and arrivals it can handle in any single time period; this envelope consists of several linear segments, each defined by a coefficient on departures, a coefficient on arrivals, and a capacity limit. Two versions of each airport's capacity envelope are provided, one for visual meteorological conditions and one for instrument meteorological conditions, with the instrument-conditions envelope being more restrictive. Each weather scenario specifies, for every airport and every time period, which of these two operating conditions prevails, and each scenario carries a known probability. A given weight parameter, rho between zero and one, governs the tradeoff between scheduling costs and expected operational delay costs.

The authority must make two kinds of decisions. In the first stage, before weather uncertainty is resolved, it must set a scheduled departure period and a scheduled arrival period for every flight. Each flight's scheduled departure period may differ from its requested departure period by at most a given maximum displacement (the same limit for all flights), and the scheduled arrival period must maintain the flight's scheduled en-route duration relative to the scheduled departure. In the second stage, after a weather scenario is realized, the authority must set an operated departure period and an operated arrival period for every flight in that scenario, along with departure and arrival delay quantities measuring how much later the operated times are compared to the scheduled times.

The scheduled times are represented through a non-increasing sequence of indicators for each flight across its scheduling window: for each period in the window, an indicator equals one if the flight is scheduled to depart no earlier than that period, and zero otherwise, so the sequence transitions from ones to zeros exactly once, and the period of that transition encodes the scheduled departure time. The same encoding applies to scheduled arrivals and, in the second stage, to operated departures and operated arrivals. The scheduling indicators must be non-increasing across consecutive periods. The difference between the sum of scheduled-arrival indicators and the sum of scheduled-departure indicators over all time periods must equal the flight's scheduled en-route duration. For every connected flight pair in the first set of connections, the difference between the sum of the second flight's scheduled-departure indicators and the sum of the first flight's scheduled-arrival indicators over all time periods must be at least the minimum connecting time for that pair.

In each weather scenario, the operated-time indicators must likewise be non-increasing across consecutive periods. The departure delay of each flight in a given scenario equals the difference between the sum of operated-departure indicators and the sum of scheduled-departure indicators over all time periods. The arrival delay of each flight in a given scenario must be at least the difference between the sum of operated-arrival indicators and the sum of scheduled-arrival indicators over all time periods. Each flight's departure delay must not exceed its maximum allowable departure delay, and its arrival delay must not exceed its maximum allowable arrival delay. For every connected flight pair in the aircraft-only connection set, the difference between the sum of the second flight's operated-departure indicators and the sum of the first flight's operated-arrival indicators must be at least the minimum connecting time for that pair. The difference between the sum of operated-arrival indicators and the sum of operated-departure indicators for each flight must lie between the flight's minimum and maximum allowable en-route durations. At each airport in each time period and each scenario, the capacity envelope must be respected: for each segment of the envelope, the segment's departure coefficient times the number of flights departing from that airport in that period plus the segment's arrival coefficient times the number of flights arriving at that airport in that period must not exceed the capacity limit for that segment under the operating condition prevailing at that airport and period in that scenario. The number of departures at an airport in a given period is determined by the drop in operated-departure indicators between consecutive periods, summed over all flights departing from that airport; arrivals are determined analogously.

The goal is to minimize a weighted combination of two cost components. The first component, weighted by rho, is the total schedule displacement cost across all flights, where the displacement cost of a flight equals the absolute difference between its scheduled and requested departure periods, extracted from the indicator encoding as the sum over periods of the displacement-cost coefficient times the drop in scheduled-departure indicators at that period. The second component, weighted by one minus rho, is the expected operational delay cost across all scenarios, where the delay cost in a given scenario is the sum over all flights of each flight's per-period departure delay cost times its departure delay plus its per-period arrival delay cost times its arrival delay, and the expectation is taken with respect to the scenario probabilities.
